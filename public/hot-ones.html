Hot Ones Got Hotter. Guests Stopped Quitting.

I analyzed 21 seasons of the internet's spiciest talk show: every sauce, every guest, every episode title. Here's what really happens in the hot seat.

By Cindy Chen

The Scoville scale, logarithmic. Each tick is ten times hotter than the last.

In my senior year of high school, I had the bright idea to force my friends to try the Hot Ones challenge with me. Ten wings, ten sauces, each hotter than the last: what wasn’t to love? We were too cheap for the official set, which ran about $20 a bottle, so each of us hunted down a cheaper sauce with a similar Scoville rating. By wing seven, my friend Ethan was chugging milk, tears in his eyes. By the end, only three of us were left.

On the actual show, celebrities almost never quit.

Since 2015, Hot Ones has put hundreds of celebrities, from Shaquille O’Neal to Jennifer Lawrence, through the same ritual: host Sean Evans asks thoughtful interview questions while the guest works through ten increasingly brutal hot sauces. I wouldn’t call myself an avid fan, but I’m definitely a casual one. There’s something deeply humanizing about watching people worth tens of millions of dollars more than you being brought to tears by a few drops of hot sauce.

Yet even in tears, almost all of them make it to the last wing. Are celebrities just tougher than a bunch of high schoolers, or is something else going on? I analyzed data covering the show’s first 21 seasons, from 2015 to 2023 and all 300 episodes, to find out what really happens at that table. The wings got much hotter. The guests stopped tapping out. And the show, with all of its fame, has gotten much bigger than the wings.

The heat hit its ceiling fast

When Hot Ones premiered with rapper Tony Yayo as its first guest, the wings were still tame by today’s standards. Season 1 started with Texas Pete, at 747 Scoville units, milder than the Tabasco on a diner table. It ended with Mad Dog 357, at 357,000, almost 500 times hotter.

The show quickly learned that pain makes good TV, and audiences agreed. Season 2 stretched to 40 episodes, and in 2016 it landed its first breakout hit, with comedy duo Key and Peele. That same season, it added Da Bomb Beyond Insanity, and Travis Kelce’s verdict after one bite was “Oh, that’s disgusting.” (Having tried it myself, I can confirm.) It isn’t technically the hottest sauce in the lineup (Scoville-wise, it’s only about 135,600, less than half of Mad Dog 357), but it tastes the most painful, and that’s exactly why it stays. Sean Evans has joked that fans won’t let it leave.

By Season 4, the show stopped borrowing its final sauce and built its own: The Last Dab, at 2,000,000 Scoville units, more than five times hotter than anything in Season 1. It came with its own ritual. Before the last wing, Evans tells each guest it’s tradition to put a little extra on, though they don’t have to. Most do anyway, and that’s when things get strange.

Steve-O finished the gauntlet and then poured sauce straight into his own eye. David Blaine turned the tenth wing into a magic trick. Sterling K. Brown answered the burn with a Shakespearean sonnet.

With that, the lineup found its shape. Each season, six sauces rotate out, and only four keep their seats: the show’s own The Classic, Los Calientes, and The Last Dab, plus Da Bomb. The house sauces come back as new versions. Da Bomb comes back exactly as it was, still terrible, every time.

The heat chart froze too. For the next 17 seasons, the hottest sauce stayed at exactly 2 million, and the average settled around 300,000. But that flat line hides something. The Last Dab has been rebuilt four times with hotter peppers, and every version still carries the same label: “2,000,000+.” In other words, the data can only say the sauce is at least that hot, not how much hotter it has become.

Almost nobody taps out

You might expect hotter sauces to mean more quitting. After all, the final wing got more than five times hotter in just three seasons. But the opposite happened. Season 1 actually had the worst record: two of its eight guests tapped out, with a 75% completion rate. Meanwhile, the last four seasons in the data, which aired from 2022 to 2023, were perfect. Every single guest made it to the end, even Jennifer Lawrence, who sobbed through most of her wings.

So why did the wings get hotter while the quitting stopped? My guess: it comes down to how guests see the show. Back in 2015, Hot Ones was still a small, scrappy web series, and plenty of its guests had likely never heard of it before Sean Evans’ invitation showed up.

Eleven years later, being on Hot Ones is like a rite of passage. The channel has more than 14 million subscribers, and each season’s sauce lineup is announced before the episodes air, so guests know what’s coming, down to Da Bomb waiting at No. 8. A lot of celebrities show up as fans, and some, like Post Malone and Gordon Ramsay, have even come back for a second round. They’ve seen the classic episodes, and they want their own moment at the table. Finishing comes with a reward, too: once the last wing is down, Evans “rolls out the red carpet” and lets guests plug whatever they’re promoting. Quitting, on the other hand, earns you a spot in the show’s Hall of Shame.

Every title follows the same formula

If you've scrolled past a Hot Ones video, you've seen the title format, even if you never noticed it.

Guest+What happened+While Eating Spicy Wings

Jennifer Lawrence Sobs in Pain While Eating Spicy Wings. Gal Gadot Does a Spit Take While Eating Spicy Wings. Stephen Curry Is On Fire While Eating Spicy Wings.

Nearly every title, 98 percent to be exact, ends with spicy wings. Only the middle changes, and that’s where the fun is. The most common opening word is “Gets,” in 36 titles. Often, the wings are the attacker: in 19 titles, a guest gets something done to them “by Spicy Wings.”

The attack is usually personal. Basketball players Kevin Love and Blake Griffin get Dunked On and Full-Court Pressed. Wrestler Chris Jericho gets Body Slammed. Roast comedian Jeff Ross gets Roasted, and Jon Bernthal, who played the Punisher, gets Punished. Somewhere at First We Feast, someone clearly has a lot of fun writing these.

And then there are the one-offs, 98 titles whose verb never appears again. Guests have Hallucinated, Achieved Enlightenment, Solved a Rubik’s Cube and Pirouetted in Pain. Aubrey Plaza Snorts Milk. And my personal favorite: Ken Jeong, a licensed doctor before he was an actor, Performs a Physical.

Why it works

Notice what almost none of the titles mention: whether the guest made it to the last wing. Hot Ones isn’t really asking whether celebrities can survive the wings, because almost all of them do. It’s watching how they survive: whether they sob, sweat, laugh, hiccup, or get a leg cramp.

So are celebrities just tougher than a bunch of high schoolers? Not really. They know exactly what’s coming, they’ve watched the classics, and they want their moment in the hot seat. Ethan, chugging milk at wing seven, didn’t have 14 million people watching or a red carpet waiting at the end. If he had, maybe he would’ve made it to wing ten too.

Methodology. Episode, guest, and sauce data come from the TidyTuesday Hot Ones dataset (August 8, 2023), compiled from Wikipedia’s list of Hot Ones episodes. It covers Seasons 1–21, from March 2015 to August 2023. Sauce Scoville ratings come from the same dataset. A guest was counted as quitting if they did not finish all ten wings, as recorded in the dataset’s “finished” field. Spin-offs like Hot Ones Versus were excluded. Two holiday specials with no listed guest were left out of the title analysis. The "what happened" part of each title was extracted in Python by removing the guest's name and the spicy-wings ending, and the first word of what remains was treated as the verb.
